3. (Essential) Three best/most important research outputs since 2021, with year, title, journal and number of authors, highlighting specific contribution. No personal details or links.

(1) 2026, "A Deep Learning Based Magnetic Field Surrogate Modeling Method for Interior Permanent Magnet Machine in EVs," accepted at the International Conference on Electrical Machines (ICEM), 3 authors. As first author I conceived the method and led the work end to end. Rather than mapping design parameters to scalar performance figures, an implicit physics-knowledge embedding approach trains a deep network, with a Fourier coordinate-encoding layer, to predict the full magnetic vector potential field over a 15-dimensional design space, so torque, flux linkage and iron loss follow from conventional finite-element post-processing. On unseen validation designs the model reaches R-squared = 0.9996 for average torque and 0.9730 for iron loss, exceeding Kriging and response-surface baselines, with inference time reduced by more than 94% against finite-element analysis on low-cost GPU hardware. The training data and solver are from an in-house Python finite-element code package I developed. This output defines the research direction I propose to develop at Bath.

(2) 2025, "Design of n-Layer Flat Wire Winding in Permanent Magnet Machine for Electric Vehicles," IEEE Transactions on Transportation Electrification, 3 authors. As first author I developed a general design method for flat-wire (hairpin) windings with any layer number, where previous methods allowed only even numbers. Odd-layer designs ease the pairing of torque-speed envelope and DC bus voltage, and the method reduces pin types and manufacturing complexity, supporting serialised production. The method was validated by finite-element analysis across five- to eight-layer designs and by two dynamometer-tested prototypes built through an industrial collaboration. The paper received a 2025 Prize Paper Award (2nd Prize) from the journal.

(3) 2026, "High-Fidelity Lumped Parameter Thermal Model for Oil-Cooling Hairpin Winding PM Machines in EVs," IEEE Transactions on Transportation Electrification, 3 authors. As first author I developed a conductor-resolved lumped-parameter thermal model coupled to a fluid-circuit representation of the oil coolant, capturing coolant mass transfer and thermal transients, with an oil-covered area model for partial wetting of the end-windings. Validated against 48 thermocouples on a dynamometer-tested prototype across multiple flow rates and operating points, it predicts temperatures to within 5 degrees Celsius in seconds of computation, where equivalent CFD is reported to need more than 120 CPU-hours, and its calibration loop uses a neural-network surrogate. The model is implemented in the same in-house Python package.

5. (Essential) What I hope to achieve within the first three years, and how it relates to computer systems engineering and artificial intelligence applied to electronic and electrical engineering.

My goal for the first three years is to establish a funded, internationally visible research group on AI-native simulation and design of electromagnetic energy-conversion devices: using deep learning to replace the computational bottleneck of finite-element analysis in electronic and electrical engineering design workflows. My published deep-learning surrogate predicts the full magnetic field of a PM machine with more than 94% inference speed-up on low-cost GPU hardware; it is an initial attempt at involving AI/ML directly in finite-element analysis for electrical machines, and the step I will take at Bath is to generalise it. Concretely, by the end of year three I aim to have: (1) extended the learned simulator from magnetostatic fields to eddy-current and PWM-driven excitation conditions and coupled electromagnetic-thermal fields, drawing on operator-learning and physics-informed methods, with the GPU-based data-generation and training pipeline built on my in-house Python finite-element code package; (2) released a benchmark dataset of structured machine-design cases to support data-centric research in the field; (3) demonstrated differentiable design, using gradient access through the learned simulator for design optimisation and manufacturing-tolerance sensitivity analysis, which conventional FE workflows cannot offer; (4) secured an EPSRC New Investigator Award and at least one industrially co-funded project, with two PhD students in post; and (5) built working links with The Foundry on digital tools for sustainable manufacturing, applying the framework to manufacturing-aware design of traction machines for transport electrification.

This programme is computer systems engineering and AI applied to electronic and electrical engineering in a direct sense: it combines GPU computing, scientific software engineering and deep learning with the electromagnetic and multi-physics analysis at the core of the discipline, and it generates computational teaching material (solver code, surrogate models, design datasets) that feeds the Department's applied computer systems and AI curriculum.

6. (Essential) Teaching experience/approach and how it is appropriate for the role.

My teaching approach centres on connecting theory to real engineering systems. Students engage with an equation differently when they can see the dynamometer trace it predicts, or step through the solver code that implements it. My research practice supplies authentic material for exactly the teaching this post asks for: applied computer systems engineering and artificial intelligence delivered through lectures and computational classes.

On experience, I served as teaching assistant on a postgraduate course in electrical machine systems (tutorial delivery, daily consultation hours, structured written feedback on student project proposals, mark recommendations), and I have sustained experience of technical mentoring of junior PhD and MSc colleagues, reflected in jointly authored publications. For computational and programming teaching, I develop and maintain an in-house Python finite-element package (object-oriented design, sparse linear algebra, transient solvers) and PyTorch deep-learning models trained on GPUs, and I program TI C2000 and STM32 motor-drive controllers in C/C++, including building and commissioning a three-phase full-bridge converter. This supports teaching of scientific programming, numerical methods, microcontroller programming and hardware-software integration. For the postgraduate artificial intelligence course, I can contribute application-focused content on deploying deep learning on physical systems: training-data design from simulation, physics-informed and operator-learning methods, and validating models against ground-truth physics.

For a specialised final-year unit I could develop "AI for Engineering Simulation and Design", in which computational classes have students train a small surrogate against a provided finite-element solver and use it inside an optimisation loop; the solver and surrogate already exist as working infrastructure. I favour mixed assessment so students with different strengths have more than one route to demonstrate learning, and I would approach curriculum work collaboratively, learning the Department's accreditation framework before proposing changes. I would undertake PHEAF on appointment.
